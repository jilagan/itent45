How work grows with the list

Some methods take twice as long when a list doubles. Others take four times as long, and some barely slow down. This lesson shows how to tell which is which.

A page of results opens at once with 10 items. With 5,000 items the same page loads slowly. The code did not change. The number of steps it takes grew with the list, and some methods grow much faster than others. How the work gets bigger as the job gets bigger is called growth.

Think first. A job takes 10 minutes for 100 items. How long for 200 items if it checks each item once? How long if it compares every pair?
The usual answers are 20 and 20, or 20 and 40. Checking each item once gives 20 minutes. Comparing every pair gives 40 minutes, four times as many comparisons. Collect the guesses before anyone opens the next step.

How the steps are counted

Job A takes n steps. Job B takes n times n steps. Job C takes one step for each halving, plus a last look at the one item left. For 64 items the list goes 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. That is 6 halvings and 1 last look, 7 steps in all.

Names for the three jobs

Computer scientists write the jobs as O(n), O(n squared) and O(log n). The notation is not needed for an estimate. Double the list and see whether the steps double, quadruple or go up by one.

Where the times come from

One question per course takes (1 + courses) times 0.4 milliseconds. One question for everything takes 0.4 milliseconds plus 0.01 milliseconds for each course sent back. At 2,000 courses that is 800 milliseconds against 20. These are teaching figures. Real times depend on the network and the database.

Why small numbers can hide the problem

A job that does 100 steps for each item is slower than one that does 3, even though both double when the list doubles. Growth shows what happens at large sizes. Measure to compare small ones. Sorting and searching shows two methods whose steps grow at very different rates. The N+1 query problem follows this case in detail.

When the list doubles, twice as many steps means the job looks at each item once. Four times as many steps means it compares every pair. One more step means it halves the list. Comparing every record with every record in a list of 100,000 takes 10 billion comparisons.

You can now count a method's steps, say what happens when the list doubles and judge whether the method will finish in reasonable time.

Growth and money

A cloud bill grows with the work done. A method that quadruples its work when users double can need four times the servers. A design that is fine at 100 users can fail at 10,000. Server capacity and Load balancers and scaling show what adding machines can and cannot fix.
