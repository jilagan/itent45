Cloud cost: capital expenditure and operating expenditure

Owning servers is capital expenditure (CapEx). Renting them is operational expenditure (OpEx).

To get around Metro Manila every day, you can buy a jeepney or book a Grab. The jeepney costs a large sum at the start and loses value every year, whether you drive it or not. Each Grab trip costs more than driving your own vehicle, but you pay only for the trips you take.

Think first. Seven servers were needed for about twenty minutes on enlistment morning, so what share of the year were they busy?
Twenty minutes is about 0.004% of a year, so the servers were idle almost all the time. Ask for guesses before moving on. The capacity chart in the third step shows the same shape.

Read the two cards. Finance usually approves cloud spending because of how each choice is recorded in the accounts.

CapEx

You own the servers

A large sum is spent up front and written off over several years.

OpEx

You rent by the hour

The cost is a monthly expense, traced to the project that used it.

More detail

What CapEx buys

Servers, a data center and enterprise licenses. The cost is predictable, but you cannot adjust it. You must guess your peak demand years ahead, and a wrong guess is expensive in either direction.

What OpEx buys

Computing time, storage and software subscriptions (SaaS). The starting cost is low and capacity can change, but the meter keeps running. A forgotten test environment is billed every month until someone notices.

Reading the chart

The area between the demand line and the capacity line is either capacity you paid for and did not use, or demand that was turned away. A low capacity setting turns demand away, and a high setting pays for capacity that sits idle.

Renting costs more per unit, but you pay only for what you use. The units in the chart are illustrative.

Total cost of ownership (TCO) includes much more than the purchase price. The price is the item most comparisons use. Here are three of the six items.

Acquisition

Purchase price, taxes, shipping and installation.

Operating

Power, cooling, bandwidth and licenses. These costs run every hour, whether or not anyone uses the system.

Downtime

The cost of the system being unavailable, planned or not. For enlistment morning this is the largest item.

You can now compare buying and renting with more than the purchase price.

More detail

The other three items

Maintenance & support

Servicing, repairs, vendor support contracts, and the software updates (patches) that someone must install, often on a weekend.

Training

Teaching staff to operate it. The cost returns each time someone leaves and a replacement is hired.

Disposal

Retiring the system, destroying data and recycling hardware. It is a real cost that is usually missing from the original business case.

Discussion question

How might a startup benefit differently from OpEx than a large enterprise would?

Instructor notes Allow about five minutes for the discussion question. Push students past "it is cheaper", because for a large enterprise with steady load, renting is often more expensive. Useful angles: who can afford to be wrong about their capacity guess, who already owns a data center they have not finished depreciating, and who needs to survive long enough to have a capacity problem at all.
