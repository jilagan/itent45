The REA pattern: resources, events and agents

Most business systems record transactions with one pattern, called REA. Because it repeats, you rarely design a schema from nothing.

At a sari-sari store, the owner hands over a bottle of soda and takes 20 pesos, or writes the amount in the utang notebook to be paid later. The goods going out and the payment coming back are two separate records.

Think first. Think of a purchase you made this week: what was given, what came back, and who was on each side?
Take two or three examples from students and name the resource, event and agents of each aloud. Lunch at the canteen and a Grab ride both work.

Each tab shows one business drawn with the same six boxes: a resource, an event and the agents on each side. The dashed line between the sides is duality. Open each tab and compare.

More detail

The three parts of REA

Resource: the thing of value, such as a seat, a book, an item of stock or cash. The event moves it.

Event: a record of something that happened at a moment and changed who holds the resource. Events are usually the tables with the most rows.

Agent: a person or organization on one side of an event. Usually one is inside your organization and one is outside it, and you need to record both.

Where the foreign keys sit

One resource appears in many events, and one agent takes part in many events. So the event table holds both foreign keys, following the rule from "Relationships" that the foreign key goes on the many side.

Why there are two events

A seat is given and money is taken. A book goes out and comes back. If you model only the half you were asked about, the system cannot answer where the money went.

The hardest business questions are gaps between two events that should have paired: unpaid enrollments, overdue books, orders delivered but not billed. "Payments and gateways" shows the same problem on the network side.

The dashed line joins two events that pair up, such as an enrollment and its payment. One payment can cover several enrollments, and one enrollment can be paid in installments. So the pairing is many-to-many and needs a table of its own. That table records how much of each payment applies to each enrollment.

The table has four rows, three enrollments and two payments. Neither side divides evenly into the other, so the amount column cannot sit in the enrollment table or in the payment table. "Relationships" explains why a many-to-many link needs a table of its own.

Events without a matching event

Some events legitimately have no partner: a scholarship waiver, a spoiled batch, a donated seat, a book written off after a flood. A resource decreases and nothing comes back. Reconciliation is the check that matches each event with its partner. If it reports every unpaired event as an exception, it produces false alarms, and people stop reading it.

Decide once which events are expected to pair, and record that decision in the model. Only a missing pair that should exist needs investigating.

Because the same shape repeats, the industry publishes ready-made templates for common domains, called universal data models. Django's user and login tables are one example. A template shows which entities other people found necessary. The one you would not have thought of is usually the event in the middle.

Where to put the boxes (optional)

A diagram of tables can be laid out in many ways, and most people place the boxes wherever there is room. One convention costs nothing: draw every crow's foot pointing up and to the left. A crow's foot is the three-pronged symbol on the many side of a relationship. The many side is almost always the entity that records what happens, so the tables that grow fastest end up in the top left, and every foreign key points down and to the right.
