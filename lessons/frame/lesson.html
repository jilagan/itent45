Framing a problem

A request such as "get us a booth" needs answers before work starts: what the team has, what it must deliver and what limits apply. A success test says when the work is done.

Your org fair committee says, "Get us a booth." Nobody says what the booth is for, how many visitors to expect, how much money there is or which day it opens. Before anyone buys tarpaulin, the team writes down what it starts with, what it must produce and what limits apply. Computational thinking calls this framing the problem.

Think first. Your org gets a 2 by 3 meter booth and 5,000 pesos. What three facts would you ask the organizers before planning anything?
Typical answers are the number of visitors, whether there is electricity and the opening hours. Collect a few before anyone moves on. The next step sorts ten facts, and three of them are unknown.

A problem has inputs, outputs and limits. Inputs are what the team starts with. Outputs are what it must deliver. Limits are rules it cannot break. Some facts are still unknown. Press a fact card, then press the box where it belongs.

A success test is a check that gives every person the same answer. It uses a count, a time or a yes or no, as in "at least 100 names on the sheet by 5 PM." In each round, press the test that anyone could check the same way.

A framed problem lists its inputs, outputs and limits, and it has a success test that anyone can check. Facts that are still unknown become questions for the organizers. A fact the team believes without checking is an assumption. Write each assumption on the plan so that someone can check it.

You can now frame a problem: name its inputs, outputs and limits, write a success test, and list what is still unknown.

More detail

Questions to ask before work starts

Ask who it is for, how many, by when, with how much money, and what happens if something goes wrong. Each answer turns a vague word into a number or a date.

Inputs and outputs in software

A sign-up form takes names and emails as inputs. It produces a confirmation message and a saved row as outputs. A limit might be that the form works on a phone with a slow connection. A web service has the same parts: a request goes in and a response comes out, as in HTTP verbs, URLs and status codes (REST).
