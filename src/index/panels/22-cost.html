Cloud cost: capital expenditure and operating expenditure

Suppose you need to get around Metro Manila every day. You can buy a jeepney. That costs a large sum at the start, and the jeepney loses value every year whether you drive it or not. Or you can book a Grab ride. Each trip costs more than driving your own vehicle, but you pay only for the trips you take.

Servers work the same way. Owning servers is capital expenditure (CapEx): a large sum spent up front, recorded as an asset, and depreciated (written off gradually) over several years, before you know how much capacity you will need. Renting servers from a cloud provider is operational expenditure (OpEx): a monthly bill for what you used, recorded as an expense when you incur it. The technical argument for the cloud is elasticity, which means capacity that grows and shrinks with demand. The argument that usually gets it approved is the accounting one.

Think first The enlistment system needed seven servers for the twenty minutes of enlistment morning. What were those seven servers doing during the rest of the year?
Asset-heavy: CapEx

You own the infrastructure

Servers, a data center and enterprise licenses. A large sum is committed up front and depreciated over several years.

The cost is predictable, but you cannot adjust it. You must guess your peak demand years ahead, and a wrong guess is expensive in either direction.
Asset-light: OpEx

You rent it by the hour

Computing time, storage and software subscriptions (SaaS). The cost is recorded as an expense each month, and you can trace it to the project that used it.

The starting cost is low and capacity can change, but the meter keeps running. A forgotten test environment is billed every month until someone notices.

Drag the capacity slider. A low setting turns demand away, and a high setting pays for capacity that sits idle. Renting costs more per unit, but you pay only for what you use.

The units are illustrative.
Total cost of ownership

Total cost of ownership (TCO) is the full cost of a system over its whole life. The purchase price is one item in it, and it is often the one that gets compared.

Acquisition

Purchase price, taxes, shipping and installation. This is the number most comparisons use.

Operating

Power, cooling, bandwidth, licenses and consumables. These costs run every hour, whether or not anyone uses the system.

Maintenance & support

Servicing, repairs, vendor support contracts, and the software updates (patches) that someone must install, often on a weekend.

Training

Teaching staff to operate it. The cost returns each time someone leaves and a replacement is hired.

Downtime

The cost of the system being unavailable, planned or not. For enlistment morning this is the largest item.

Disposal

Retiring the system, destroying data and recycling hardware. It is a real cost that is usually missing from the original business case.

Discussion question How might a startup benefit differently from OpEx than a large enterprise would?
Instructor notes Allow about five minutes. Push students past "it is cheaper", because for a large enterprise with steady load, renting is often more expensive. Useful angles: who can afford to be wrong about their capacity guess, who already owns a data center they have not finished depreciating, and who needs to survive long enough to have a capacity problem at all.
