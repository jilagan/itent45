Training: adjusting a model to reduce its errors

Training repeats one routine: measure how wrong the line is, nudge it, and measure again. This lesson shows how the line is found and what the size of each nudge does.

Suppose you are standing on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the bottom. You cannot see it, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you step downhill, feel the slope again and repeat.

Think first. Each step downhill has a size. What goes wrong if every step is very large? What goes wrong if every step is very small? You will test both next.

Press Run 200 and watch the loss fall as the line settles. Then move the step size, also called the learning rate, to its largest setting, press Start over and run 200 steps again.

Choosing the learning rate

The learning rate is the size of each step, and somebody has to choose it. If it is too small, training crawls, and you pay for computing power that is barely moving the line. If it is too large, the line jumps past the bottom and ends up on the other side, over and over, and the loss goes up when it should go down.

Nothing detects this for you. It is the most common reason a training run produces nothing, and it is the setting beginners change most often.

The line you just moved has three numbers. A weight sets how much hours studied counts, another weight does the same for attendance, and the bias shifts the line. Each step nudges all three to lower the loss. Nobody writes the final line down.

More detail

The terms on this screen

Loss is one score for how wrong the line is across all of the students. Lower is better. Cross-entropy is the formula that produces the loss, and it gives a large penalty when the model is confident and wrong. Gradient descent is the routine of working out the slope of the loss for each of the three numbers and moving each one a small step downhill.

Scale

A large language model does the same kind of adjusting to hundreds of billions of numbers, for months, on thousands of machines. The arithmetic is similar, and the cost is far larger.
