Training data and test data

A model is built from examples and then judged on examples it has not seen. This lesson fits a line to some students and tests it on others.

Suppose you are given the exam paper a week early and memorize every answer. A model faces the same problem. It is built from examples, and it still has to be judged on how well it works.

Think first. You see the exam a week early and score 100 percent. What does that score tell anyone about your understanding? How would you test it fairly?

What this kind of model is called

A straight boundary fitted to labeled examples is called logistic regression. The name confuses many people, because regression usually means predicting a number, and this model predicts a category.

Linear regression draws a line through the points to predict a value, such as the mark a student will get from the hours studied. Logistic regression draws a line between the points to predict a group, such as pass or fail. On screen both are a line. The first answers "how much?" and the second answers "which group?". The later lessons use the second kind.

How a model becomes a boundary

Nobody writes the line down. It starts in a poor position and is adjusted until it makes fewer mistakes. Adjusting a model to match examples is called fitting it, and the final line is the model.

Split the data before fitting, and only once. If you look at the test set, adjust something and test again, you have trained on it. That mistake is called leakage.

Why a good training score means little

A model that has seen every answer can remember them, and from the inside remembering looks the same as learning. Every adjustment is made to reduce mistakes on the training examples, so a good score there is expected.

Why leakage is easy to miss

People make this mistake often without noticing, and results that looked strong during development then fall apart in front of real users. Information from the test set leaks into the model, which is where the name comes from.

Reading the gap

A small gap between the two scores means the model found the general shape of the data. A large gap means it memorized its examples. The bent line in "Fit a line and test it" shows this directly. A test set is commonly 20 to 30 percent of the data.
