Training: adjusting a model to reduce its errors

Suppose you are standing on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the bottom. You cannot see the bottom, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel the slope again, and repeat.

Training a model follows the same routine. Start with a line placed at random. Measure how wrong it is with a single number called the loss. Nudge the line in whichever direction makes the loss smaller, and repeat a few hundred times. The fog stands for the fact that the program never sees the best line in advance and only measures the slope where it is standing.

The panel "Training and test data" used a boundary line without showing where it came from. This panel shows how the line is found.

Think first. Each step downhill in the fog has a size. What could go wrong if every step is very large? What could go wrong if every step is very small? Write down your guesses, then test them with the step size control below.

The terms on this screen Loss, cross-entropy and gradient descent are the standard terms for this process. Here is what each one means on this screen.

Loss is the number in the first box. It is one score for how wrong the line is across all of the students. Lower is better.

Cross-entropy is the formula that produces the loss. It gives a large penalty when the model is confident and wrong.

Gradient descent is the routine of working out the slope of the loss for each of the three numbers the model adjusts, and moving each one a small step downhill.
Three numbers to adjust This line has a weight for hours, a weight for attendance, and a bias. A weight sets how much one measurement counts, and the bias moves the line up or down. Training adjusts those three numbers. A large language model does the same kind of adjusting to hundreds of billions of numbers, for months, on thousands of machines. The arithmetic is similar, and the cost is far larger.
Choosing the learning rate The learning rate is the size of each step, and somebody has to choose it. If it is too small, training crawls, and you pay for computing power that is barely moving the line. If it is too large, the line jumps past the bottom and ends up on the other side, over and over, and the loss goes up when it should go down. Nothing detects this for you. It is the most common reason a training run produces nothing, and it is the setting beginners change most often.

Select the largest setting and watch the loss curve go up.
