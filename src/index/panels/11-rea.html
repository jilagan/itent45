The REA pattern: resources, events and agents

Think of a sale at a sari-sari store. The owner hands over a bottle of soda and takes 20 pesos, or writes the amount in the utang notebook to be paid later. The goods going out and the payment coming back are two separate records.

Most business systems follow this same pattern, called REA, for resources, events and agents. A resource is something of value, such as the soda or the cash. An event records that a resource changed hands, such as the sale. An agent is a person or organization taking part, such as the owner or the customer.

The sale and the payment form a pair called duality: value moving one way is usually paid for by value moving the other way. Because the pattern repeats, you rarely design a schema, the layout of a system's tables, from nothing.

Think first. Pick a transaction you made this week, such as buying lunch or paying for a ride. Write down what was given, what came back, and who was on each side.

Each tab shows one business case drawn with the same six boxes: on each side, a resource, an event and the agents.

Payments and enrollments

In an REA diagram, the dashed line joins two events that pair up, such as an enrollment and its payment. It is tempting to read the line as meaning that every enrollment has exactly one payment, and to store that in a column. That fails when a student pays in installments, or pays for several subjects with one transfer. The pairing is many-to-many, so it needs a table of its own, as described in "Relationships". That table must record how much of this payment applies to that enrollment.

Events without a matching event Some events legitimately have no partner: a scholarship waiver, a spoiled batch, a donated seat, a book written off after a flood. A resource decreases and nothing comes back. Reconciliation is the check that matches each event with its partner. If it reports every unpaired event as an exception, it produces false alarms, and people stop reading it.

Decide once which events are expected to pair, and record that decision in the model so that it is written down. Only a missing pair that should exist needs investigating.
Where to put the boxes

A diagram of tables can be laid out in many ways, and most people place the boxes wherever there is room. The result is hard to read a second time. One convention costs nothing: draw every crow's foot pointing up and to the left. A crow's foot is the three-pronged symbol on the many side of a relationship. The many side is almost always the entity that records what happens, so the tables that grow fastest end up in the top left.

The three parts of REA
Resource: the thing of value, such as a seat, a book, an item of stock or cash. The event moves it.

Event: a record of something that happened at a moment and changed who holds the resource. Events are usually the tables with the most rows.

Agent: a person or organization on one side of an event. Usually one is inside your organization and one is outside it, and you need to record both.
Why there are two events
The pairing is called duality. A seat is given and money is taken. A book goes out and comes back. If you model only the half you were asked about, the system cannot answer where the money went.

Duality also produces the hardest business questions: unpaid enrollments, overdue books, orders delivered but not billed. Each one is a gap between two events that should have paired and did not.
Universal data models Because the same shape repeats, the industry publishes ready-made templates for common domains. These are called universal data models. The frameworks you will use include their own versions, such as Django's user and login tables, a Supabase starter schema or a ready-made set of tables for an online store. Each is somebody else's solution to a model you were about to draw yourself.

Use them, and read them. A template shows which entities other people found necessary, and the one you would not have thought of is usually the event in the middle.
