Sorting and searching

Putting a list in order, called sorting, takes work once. After that, any item can be found in a few guesses.

In a shopping app you tap Sort by price, and 40 listings reorder. In your contacts you look for one name in a list that is already in alphabetical order. In a sorted list you can open the middle, see which half holds the name and repeat on that half.

Think first. You must find one ID in 1,000 unsorted entries and in 1,000 sorted entries. About how many entries do you check in each list?
Most students say 1,000 for both, or 500 for the unsorted list on average. Collect guesses before step 3. The sorted answer is 10.

How few comparisons are enough?

Eight items can be arranged in 40,320 ways. Each comparison has two possible results, so 15 comparisons can separate at most 32,768 orders. That is fewer than 40,320, so 16 comparisons are the least that always works. Comparing every pair of items takes 28.

In a sorted list, one guess can rule out half of the cells. Finding an item this way is called binary search. IDs rise from left to right.

Press a cell to see its ID. Keep going until you reach the ID you want.

Each guess halves the cells still open. A list of 15 needs at most 4 guesses, 31 needs 5 and 63 needs 6. Doubling the list adds one guess.

Sorting costs work once, so it pays off for a list that is searched many times.

Guesses for larger lists

A list of 1,000 needs at most 10 guesses. One million needs 20, and one billion needs 30.

When sorting is not worth it

Sorting 500 items takes thousands of comparisons. A list searched only once is faster to scan from the start.

In databases

An index is a sorted copy of one column, so the database can halve its search. Without an index, a search reads every row, as in The N+1 query problem. SQL basics shows how queries ask for rows.
