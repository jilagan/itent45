Attention and the transformer

Attention lets each word in a sentence look at the other words and decide which ones matter. A transformer is a model built around this idea.

In a busy group chat, a reply such as "yes, that one" only makes sense if you look back and decide which earlier message it answers. Attention works the same way. Each word looks back at the other words and decides which ones matter for it.

Think first. In The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired, what does it refer to? Change tired to wide. Does the answer change?
Ask students what they looked at in the sentence to decide. The answer in both cases is the last word, and the widget shows the model doing the same thing.

Each bar is a weight. Click a highlighted word, or switch the last word between tired and wide, and watch where it points. The weights are set by hand to show the idea.

More detail

What the word asks for and what the others offer

The weight is a similarity score between a question and a description. In a real model, both are positions in the space described in "Vectors and similarity", and the match is a distance calculated fresh for each sentence. The short descriptions in quotes are our own words for what those positions encode. The model holds positions and holds no sentences about meaning.

Every token is compared with every other token, so the number of comparisons is the square of the length. Move the slider and watch the comparisons grow.

Older models squeezed everything read so far into one small note, and early words faded. A transformer keeps every word, so word 1 and word 5,000 are one step apart. All the comparisons happen at once, so training can be spread across many chips.

More detail

The older approach

"Recurrent networks" showed the single note that was rewritten after each word, where animal had faded by the time the sentence needed it. Attention keeps every word and lets each one look at all the others directly.

Where the name comes from

The T in GPT stands for transformer, and GPT stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer. The 2017 paper that introduced the design was titled Attention Is All You Need.

Doubling the prompt

Doubling the input roughly quadruples the comparisons, so the cost of long prompts rises faster than the number of tokens.
