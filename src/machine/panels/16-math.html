The math behind it: vectors, slopes and probabilities

Your grade in a course is a weighted sum. You multiply each score by its weight and add the results. That one operation is the main piece of math in language models. This panel covers three ideas that appear in every lecture and paper on the topic: the dot product, the slope and the probability distribution. Each is school arithmetic, and each connects to a screen you have already seen. The symbols in papers are shorthand for these steps.

Think first. Before you read on, write down what you think each of these means: a dot product, a slope, a probability distribution. Then compare your answers with the explanations below.
1 · Vectors and the dot product

Suppose a course grades exams at weight 0.6 and projects at weight 0.4, and you score 80 on exams and 90 on projects. Your grade is 80 × 0.6 + 90 × 0.4 = 84. You multiplied each pair of numbers and added the results to get one number. That is a dot product. A vector is a list of numbers, so your scores [80, 90] are a vector and the weights [0.6, 0.4] are another. Two numbers can mark a position on a page. Three hundred numbers mark a position in a space you cannot draw, which is where words are placed in "Vectors and similarity".

In the picture, vector a is fixed and you swing vector b. The dot product is large when the two arrows point the same way. It is zero at a right angle and negative when they point apart.

Where you have met it The attention weight in "Attention and transformers" is a dot product between what one word is asking for and what another word offers. The similarity score in "Vectors and similarity" is the same operation with the lengths divided out, which is called cosine similarity. A unit in "Neural networks" multiplies each input by a weight and adds the results, which is a dot product with a fixed number called a bias added at the end.

A matrix is a grid of numbers, which you can read as a stack of vectors. Multiplying by a matrix performs many dot products at once. This is the meaning of the notation Wx: W is the matrix of weights and x is the input vector.
2 · Slopes and derivatives

A car's speedometer tells you how fast your position is changing right now. A slope is the same idea for any curve: how fast the answer changes when you nudge the input. A derivative is the formal name for the slope at one point. In this picture the number you control is a weight, which is a number the model can adjust. The slope is negative on the left of the dip, positive on the right, and zero at the bottom. To get downhill, move in the direction opposite to the slope.

A gradient is this same slope worked out for every number at once. With three numbers it is three slopes. With a billion numbers it is a billion slopes, and the arithmetic for each one is the same.
Where you have met it The One step button in "How training works" does exactly this. It works out the slope for each of the three numbers, moves each one against its slope, and repeats. The learning-rate slider sets how far each move goes.
3 · Probability distributions and loss

A weather forecast might say 60% rain, 30% cloudy and 10% clear. That is a distribution: a set of outcomes, each with a probability, adding up to 100%. The model's choice of next word in "Predicting the next word" is a distribution over words. A loss is one number that says how wrong the model was, given what turned out to be true.

The true answer here is Paris. The loss is small when the model put most of its probability on Paris and large when it put little there. It rises steeply as that probability approaches zero.

The formula is -log(p), where p is the probability given to the right answer and log is the logarithm function on a calculator. This loss is called cross-entropy. It turns "how much probability did the model put on the right answer" into one number that training can push down.
Where you have met it This is the number that falls in "How training works". Temperature, in "Predicting the next word", changes the shape of the distribution the model samples from. The loss measures how much probability sits on the right answer.
What you can skip for now This panel leaves out matrix decompositions, the mechanics of the chain rule (how slopes combine through a chain of steps), probability theory beyond distributions, and proofs. None of them changes a decision you will make this semester, and you can learn them later if you want to.

The three ideas above are enough to read a diagram in a lecture, follow the pictures in a paper's method section, and recognize which symbol is doing the work.
