Word embeddings: how words get positions

A word's embedding is its position on a map, found by reading sentences. Words that appear together end up close together.

At a barkada party, nobody hands out seat numbers, yet people who keep talking to each other end up sitting together. Words find their positions in a similar way. Words that appear in the same sentences end up close together on a map.

Think first. Suppose words in the same sentence move closer together. Where would you expect king and queen to end up, and where king and hospital?
Collect guesses before anyone presses Run the training. Most students expect king and queen to be close and king and hospital to be far apart.

Every word starts at a random spot. Each sentence moves its words a little closer together and pushes all other words slightly apart. Press Run the training and watch the groups form.

Each step is small and fixed

Each sentence moves the words by the same small step, so no word chooses where it goes. The map has two directions, called dimensions, so each embedding is two numbers. Real models use hundreds of numbers per word.

Look at the list. These fourteen sentences were the only input. It holds no dictionary and no labels, and nobody marked which words are about royalty. The colors on the map were added afterward to show the groups.

How this differs from real training

This is the idea behind a method called word2vec, run small enough to watch. Real training uses billions of words and hundreds of dimensions, where this demo uses fourteen sentences and two. In real text each word also appears in far more varied sentences.

A corpus this small produces the clusters. It cannot produce the finer structure behind the word arithmetic in Vectors and similarity, because that needs the same word in thousands of different contexts.
