Four terms, each one a smaller thing inside the last

People use these terms as though they were interchangeable, and vendors are happy to let them. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you what the system can do, how it fails, and whether it learned anything at all.

Ask the room. A chess program that beat the world champion in 1997. Was that artificial intelligence? It was. Did it learn anything? Not one thing.
Where does each of these sit?
Click one and watch where it lands. Two of them are artificial intelligence without being machine learning at all, which is the distinction that goes missing most often.
The four, in plain terms
Outermost
Artificial intelligence

Any program doing something we would call intelligent if a person did it. Search, planning, rules, logic. Most of it was written by hand and learned nothing, and most of it is decades old.

chess engines · route finding · scheduling
Inside that
Machine learning

The part that works out its own rule from examples instead of being given one. The rule was never written down. Somebody supplied a million labelled emails and the program found the boundary itself.

spam filters · credit scoring · recommendations
Inside that
Deep learning

Machine learning built from many stacked layers, where the layers work out for themselves what is worth measuring. You stop choosing the features and hand it the raw input.

face recognition · speech to text · translation
Innermost
Generative AI

The part that produces a new artifact rather than a verdict. A spam filter answers with one bit. This answers with a paragraph, an image, or a working function.

language models · image models · coding assistants
Three things worth being clear about before the rest of this AI is not new. The term was coined in 1956. What changed recently is one small region at the centre of that diagram.

Most AI does not learn. A great deal of what runs your bank, your airline and your traffic lights is rules somebody wrote, and it works, and nobody markets it any more.

When a product says AI today, it almost always means the innermost circle. Check it rather than assume, because the four behave very differently when they are wrong. A rule that is wrong is wrong the same way every time. A model that is wrong is wrong in a way that depends on what it was shown.

The rest of these panels are about that innermost circle, and they work outwards: what it predicts, how it is fitted, what it reads, and what happens when you give it tools.
01 — What it is doing

It is guessing the next word, and there is no second mechanism

Every answer you will ever get from one of these models is produced the same way: look at everything so far, work out a probability for every word that could come next, pick one, then do it again. There is no plan, no draft, and no separate step where it checks whether the sentence is true.

Ask the room. Finish this: the capital of France is. Everyone answered. Did you look it up, or did the sentence just end that way?
How adventurous should it be?
Temperature is how far down the list it is willing to reach. At 0 it takes the top word every single time. At 1 it samples in proportion to the numbers you see.

Run ten samples at 0, then ten at 1.2, and watch what changes.
Now do that eight hundred times

One word is a party trick. What makes it generative is the loop: take the word it picked, glue it onto the end, and ask the same question again. The answer you get back was built this way, one commitment at a time.

What is different about the loop
After the first word, the model is reading its own output. Nothing in the context marks which part you wrote and which part it wrote. They are the same kind of text by the time the next word is chosen.

It also never goes back. Once a word is on the end it is a fact the next word has to live with, and there is no step where the model reconsiders a sentence it has already started.
This is where a wrong answer becomes a confident paragraph Pick a slightly wrong word early and everything after it is chosen to be consistent with that word. The result reads smoothly all the way to the end, because smoothness is the thing being optimized at every step.
Generative, as against the thing in panel 01 The boundary in panel 01 answers a closed question: which side is this student on? One bit out, chosen from a set that existed before the model ran. Nothing new is made.

This panel answers an open one, and the set of possible answers is every sequence of words there is. That distinction is what people mean by generative. The output can be fluent, novel and wrong at the same time. A classifier can only be wrong in the ways you allowed for.
Images do not work like this at all Students assume one mechanism sits behind everything, so say this out loud. Image models mostly start from noise and remove it in steps until a picture is left, which is a different process with different failure modes. Nothing you learn about tokens here transfers to why a generated hand has six fingers.
Temperature 0 is not correct mode It is most-likely mode. If the top of that list happens to be wrong, temperature 0 gives you the same wrong answer every time you ask, worded identically and just as confidently. That is harder to catch than an answer that wobbles, not easier.
02 — How anything learns at all

A model is a line somebody found. Whether it learned anything is a separate question.

Before language models, the simplest useful thing you could do was this: collect examples of something that already happened, then find the boundary that separates the outcomes. The boundary is never written down anywhere. It gets nudged until it stops being wrong so often, and where it lands is the model.

The difficulty is that a model which has seen every answer can simply remember them, and remembering looks exactly like learning from the inside. So before you fit anything, you hide some of your data from yourself.

What is happening
What this is called A straight boundary fitted to labelled examples is logistic regression, and the name misleads almost everyone. Regression normally means predicting a number, and this predicts a category.

Linear regression draws a line through the points to predict a value: given hours studied, what mark? The answer is a number, and the line passes among the data.

Logistic regression, which is what you have here, draws a line between the points to predict a group: pass or fail. Same shape on screen, different question, and it is worth telling them apart early because everything after this is the second kind.
The two words you will hear constantly
Training set

The part the model is allowed to see while it is being fitted. Every adjustment is made to reduce mistakes on these, so a good score here is expected and means very little on its own.

Test set

The part you hid before you started, and did not look at once. It stands in for everyone the model has not met yet. Its score is the only one worth quoting.

The split has to happen before the fitting, and only once If you look at the test set, adjust something, and try again, you have quietly trained on it. It is no longer a test, and you have no honest number left. People do this constantly without noticing. Results that looked strong in development then fall apart in front of real users.

The gap between the two scores is the diagnostic. A small gap means it found the shape of the thing. A large one means it memorized the examples, which the next screens will show you directly.
03 — Where the line comes from

The line gets pushed until it stops being wrong so often

The last panel said a model is a line somebody found, and left out the finding. Here it is. Start from a line drawn at random, measure how badly it is doing, then nudge it in whatever direction makes that number smaller. Repeat a few hundred times. That is training, and there is no more to it than that.

This is the whole of it A unit is a line. A layer is a set of lines. Depth is layers feeding layers, so later ones combine what earlier ones found. Nobody hand-draws the shape at the bottom of this screen. Training moves each line until the combination stops being wrong so often.
Two pictures of the same object Most people meet neural networks as the circles-and-arrows diagram and never see what it does, so the diagram becomes a logo rather than an explanation. It is the wiring: which number gets multiplied by which weight and passed to where.

The plot underneath is the effect: what that wiring does to the space of possible students. One circle in the middle row is one line on the plot. Four circles, four lines. The weights on the two arrows entering a circle set that line's angle; its bias sets how far out it sits. Three numbers, exactly as in panel 03.

Neither picture is more correct. The diagram tells you what is connected, and the plot tells you what the connections bought you, and people who only ever see the first one end up believing the second is somehow deeper than it is.
This is no longer regression of any kind One line fitted to data is logistic regression, which is what the last two panels were. The moment you use several lines and combine their answers, you have a neural network with one hidden layer.

The vocabulary maps onto what is on screen. Each line is a unit, sometimes called a neuron. The set of them is a layer. The step that takes their answers and produces one verdict is the output unit, and here it says pass only when every line agrees.

Hidden means only that you never see what those units decided. You gave the network two numbers and read one answer out; whatever the units settled on in between is not shown to you and was not chosen by anyone.
What you gave up to get that shape
Before deep learning

You picked the features yourself. To spot a dropout you would decide what to measure: hours, attendance, whether they had missed two submissions in a row. Getting those right was the job, and it took someone who knew the domain. When the model failed you could point at which feature was wrong.

After

You hand it raw input and enough units, and the early layers work out their own features. In an image model the first layers end up finding edges, later ones shapes, later still faces. The word "edge" appears nowhere in the design. It was the most useful thing for that layer to notice.

The bill arrives later You no longer know what the features are. The eight numbers on this screen are legible; a real model has billions, and no one of them means anything on its own. So a model can be excellent and unexplainable at the same time, and when it goes wrong there is often nothing to point at.

Keep that in mind for panel 07, on where meaning comes from. The same property that lets a model find features nobody named also lets it absorb patterns nobody intended.
05 — Assigning blame

How do you get a slope for a weight that never touches the output?

Panel 03 nudged three numbers downhill, and each one touched the answer directly, so working out its slope was one step of arithmetic. The last panel introduced hidden units, and that trick stops working. A weight in the middle affects the answer only through everything in front of it. So how does training know which way to move it, and by how much?

Ask the room. A dish comes back to the kitchen wrong. Six people touched it. How much of that is the sauce station's fault? You cannot answer without knowing what each step passed to the next.
What is happening
The name is the description Going forward, each layer hands its answer to the next. Going backward, each layer hands its share of the blame to the one behind it. That is backpropagation: propagating the error backward.

People call the underlying rule the chain rule. You can use the idea without it: to know how much a weight mattered, multiply how much its output mattered by how much it affected that output.
Why it is cheap, and why that decided everything Look at the numbers as they appear. Every one is built from a number already worked out to its right. Nothing is computed twice, and nothing is computed from scratch.

The obvious alternative is to nudge each weight a little, run the whole network again, and see whether the loss improved. That works, and for a network with a billion weights it means a billion full runs to make one step. Backpropagation gets every slope in one backward pass, costing about the same as one forward pass.

The idea was published in 1986 and the arithmetic has not changed since. What changed was the hardware, and then the amount of text.
What you can skip The derivation, the notation, and the proof that it is correct. You will not implement this. Every framework does it for you, and the reason it is worth two minutes is narrower: when training produces nothing, one of the usual causes is that the blame stopped flowing backward somewhere, and people talk about that in these words.

08 — How words get compared

Meaning turns into a position, and positions can be subtracted

Tokens are numbers, and numbers do not have meanings. So each one becomes a vector: a position in a space with a few hundred directions. Words that turn up in similar company end up in similar places. There is no labelling step anywhere in it.

09 — What came before

One word at a time, and one note to itself, rewritten after every word

Here is how this was done until 2017, and it is worth two minutes because the next panel only makes sense as an answer to it. The model reads left to right. After each word it updates a single small note summarizing everything so far, then throws away the word and moves on. The note is a fixed size, so everything new that goes in pushes something old out.

Ask the room. Read the whole sentence, then ask the model what it referred to. The catch is that the sentence is no longer available to it.

What is left on the note

Every word that went in, and how much of it survived. This is not a list of answers.

Only two words could be the answer

it stands for a thing, so the candidates are the two nouns. Nothing else in the sentence can be what it meant.

The note is one fixed size
Whatever the sentence length, the note stays the same size. A short sentence fits comfortably. A paragraph does not, and nothing warns you which parts got squeezed out.

This is what people mean by a recurrent network: the same small step applied over and over, each time taking the previous note and the next word, and producing a new note.
Two problems, and the second is worse Distance. Early words fade, so anything depending on a long-range connection gets lost.

Order. Word fifty cannot be read until word forty-nine has been. Training could not be spread across many machines, which put a ceiling on how much text anyone could train on.
Both problems have the same fix Stop summarizing. Keep every word, and let each one look directly at all the others, weighing them by how well they fit rather than by how recently they arrived. Every word is then one hop from every other, and all the comparisons can happen at once instead of in sequence.

That is the next panel, and the reason this one exists.

14 — Who actually runs things

The model never touches your database. It writes a note asking someone else to.

The last panel gave the agent a shell and a test runner and called it a loop. That skipped something worth knowing before you let one near your repository: at no point does the model execute anything. It emits a request, in text, and a separate program decides whether to carry it out.

Ask the room. If the model cannot run anything itself, where exactly is the point at which somebody could say no?
What the model was given
This is the whole of what it knows about the tool: a name, a sentence, and the shape of the arguments. It has never seen your schema, your rows or your server.

So it picks a tool the way you pick one from a menu you have not tasted: by reading the description. A vague description gets the wrong tool called, and that is a bug in the description, not the model.
Why there is a standard for this

Every AI application needs to reach the same systems: your files, your database, your issue tracker, your calendar. Before there was a common format, each pairing needed its own adapter, and the count multiplies.

One adapter per pairing
One standard, implemented once each

That is what MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is for. Anthropic published it as an open standard in November 2024 and other vendors adopted it. A server wraps a system and advertises what it offers. A client, sitting inside the application you are using, asks for that list and relays calls. Write the server once and any client that speaks the protocol can use it.

Three things this buys you, and one it does not A place to say no. Every call passes through the client before it reaches anything real, so approval, logging and refusal all have somewhere to live.

A list you control. The model can only ask for tools it was offered. If it says it cannot read your file, the usual reason is that nobody gave it a tool that reads files.

Portability. The same server works in whichever editor or agent you move to next.

What it does not buy you is judgement. A tool that deletes rows will delete rows when called correctly. The protocol carries the request faithfully; deciding whether the request should have been made is still yours, and in an agent loop the requests arrive faster than you can read them.

16 — If the notation has been bothering you

Three pieces of mathematics, and you have already used all of them

Nothing in this tool needed you to know any of this. Every panel showed the idea working before naming it. But the moment you open a lecture or a paper you will meet the symbols, and the symbols are the reason people decide the field is closed to them. Here is the short version: three operations, all arithmetic you already did at school, each one tied to a screen you have already seen.

Ask the room. How much of this looks like new mathematics, and how much looks like something you did in third year high school with a different name on it?
1 · Linear algebra, which is mostly one operation

A vector is a list of numbers. That is the whole definition. Two numbers make a position on a page; three hundred make a position in a space you cannot draw, which is where words live in panel 07. The operation you do with them constantly is the dot product: multiply the pairs, add the results, get one number.

A matrix is a stack of vectors, and multiplying by one performs many of these at once. That is all the notation Wx means.

The slope is negative on the left of the dip and positive on the right, and it passes through zero at the bottom. So the rule for getting downhill needs no cleverness: move the opposite way from the slope.

A gradient is this same slope worked out for every number at once, instead of one at a time. With three numbers it is three slopes. With a billion it is a billion, and the arithmetic per number does not change.

A distribution is a set of outcomes with a number attached to each, and the numbers add to 1. Panel 01 was a distribution over next words. A loss is one number saying how wrong the whole distribution is, given what actually turned out to be true.

Two words that get used interchangeably and should not be
Vibe coding

Describe what you want, take what comes back, run it, describe the next thing. You are steering by whether it seems to work. The term is Andrej Karpathy's, and he meant it approvingly for throwaway work: projects where the cost of being wrong is that you delete the folder.

Agentic coding

The agent has a shell, your repo and your test suite, and works in the loop from panel 06. It can run things, read failures and revise. More capable, and more able to do damage without being asked, since now it acts rather than suggests.

The trouble starts when the prototype ships Vibe coding is a reasonable way to find out whether an idea is worth building. It stops being reasonable at the moment somebody depends on the result, and that moment usually arrives without an announcement. Very few people decide to put unreviewed code into production. It arrives there because the prototype worked, the demo went well, and no obvious moment came along to stop and read it.

Module 3 is largely about finding that point on purpose.
