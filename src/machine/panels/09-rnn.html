Recurrent networks: the approach before transformers

A recurrent network reads one word at a time and keeps one small note about everything read so far. Until about 2017, language models worked this way.

Imagine sitting through a long lecture with a single sticky note. After each sentence you rewrite the note to summarize everything so far, and then you forget the sentence. At the end you can answer questions only from the note. The model's note is called its hidden state.

Think first. In The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired, what does it refer to? Which words would a short summary keep most clearly?
Ask which words a rewritten note would keep best. Most students say the most recent ones, which the widget confirms.

Press Read the next word and watch the note. Each new word goes on at full strength and everything older fades. When the sentence is finished, press the button once more to ask what it refers to.

The note is one fixed size

The note stays the same size however long the sentence is. A short sentence fits comfortably. A paragraph does not, and nothing tells you which parts were squeezed out.

Why the network is called recurrent

The same small step is applied over and over. Each time, it takes the previous note and the next word and produces a new note.

Why the answer is street

The two candidates for it are the nouns animal and street, because it stands for a thing. The note holds street more strongly in both sentences, so the model answers street whichever last word it read.

Distance. Early words fade, so anything that depends on a connection across a long gap is lost. Order. Word fifty cannot be read until word forty-nine has been read, so training cannot be spread across many machines. A fifty-page contract shows both problems.

One fix for both problems

Keep every word, and let each word look directly at all the others, weighing them by how well they fit. Every word is then one step from every other word, and all the comparisons can happen at once. This approach is covered in "Attention and transformers".
