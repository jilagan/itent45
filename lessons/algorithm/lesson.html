Writing steps for a robot

A computer follows exact steps and cannot guess. A robot that makes milk tea shows what exact means and how a repeat serves a line of any length.

A new crew member at a milk tea shop follows the recipe card word for word. If the card says "add sugar," the crew member has to ask how much. A card that works for someone who cannot guess lists exact steps, in order, and ends. Such a list is an algorithm.

Think first. A robot barista knows only: take cup, add tea, add milk, add pearls, add ice, seal, serve. Write the steps for one pearl milk tea with ice.
Expect most students to put the seal before the pearls or the ice. Ask what the robot does when it reaches a step it cannot do.

    What the robot checks

    The robot stops at the first step it cannot do. It checks four rules.

    • Take cup comes first, and the robot holds one cup at a time.
    • Nothing can be added after Seal.
    • Serve needs a sealed cup.
    • The cup must hold exactly the items in the order, with each item added once.

    Why exact steps matter

    A computer does what the instruction says, including when the writer meant something else. The three common mistakes are a missing step, steps in the wrong order, and steps that never end.

    Six customers wait, and each needs the same five steps. A loop repeats a group of steps, and a rule says when to stop. Choose a rule and press Run line. Then add a customer and run again.

      Three building blocks

      Most programs combine three things: steps in order, a repeat, and a choice between steps based on a condition. Decision rules and tables covers conditions.

      A repeat that never ends

      A rule such as "Until the line is empty" stops only if each pass removes a customer. A group of steps that leaves the line unchanged runs forever.

      The same rule holds for any reader that follows instructions. "Make the form better" leaves an AI assistant to guess. "Keep only name, email and phone" gives it exact steps and a way to check the result.

      You can now write steps in order, repeat them for a line of any length, and predict what a program does by running it step by step.
