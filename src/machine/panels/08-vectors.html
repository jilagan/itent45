Vectors and similarity of meaning

On a map, two pins that are close together mark places that are near each other. Words can be treated the same way. A token is only an ID number, and an ID number carries no meaning, so each token is converted into a vector: a list of numbers that gives the word a position. Real models use a few hundred numbers per word, so their map has a few hundred directions. This list of numbers is the word's embedding. Words that appear in similar company end up in similar places, as the previous panel, "Word embeddings: how words get positions", showed. Nobody labels the words.

Think first. Which other words would you want a search for enroll to find? Why might a computer place those words near enroll without anyone telling it they mean the same thing?
Before you read the map The sixteen words on this map were placed by hand so the relationships come out cleanly. The previous panel showed the real process on a corpus small enough to watch. This panel skips ahead to what a large corpus produces. A real embedding has hundreds of dimensions, and its positions are learned from text. The map is a diagram of the idea, and the word arithmetic was set up to work.

Each button shows a sum of three word positions: the first word, minus the second, plus the third. The circle marked result is where the sum ends up, and the nearest word to it is the answer.

A detail that is often left out
Turn this option off to see which word is nearest to the result. The well-known answer only appears when the three input words are removed from the candidates, and this rule is rarely mentioned.

The pattern exists in real models. These word positions were placed by hand to show it.
Newer models give one vector per use of a word In this panel, bank would get a single point, somewhere between the river and the money. Modern models give a word a different vector depending on the sentence around it. That change is a main reason retrieval, which means finding the passages most relevant to a question, works as well as it does today.
What to take from the bias example Embeddings absorb the statistics of the text they were built from. That matters when the space is used inside a hiring screen or a scholarship filter. The famous analogies also overstate how cleanly this can be read off, because the example was arranged to work.

A model reflects the text it was built from. A prompt cannot remove that, because it is built into the positions the model uses. Anyone who claims a single tidy number for how biased a model is should be asked how they measured it.
Both statements hold, and students tend to keep only one of them: embeddings do absorb the statistics of their corpus, and the famous analogies overstate how cleanly that shows up.
Similarity between sentences

A sentence also gets a position, worked out from the words in it. Once two sentences are positions, how alike they are depends on the angle between their arrows. Pick any two sentences and compare the score with the number of words they share.

The score is the cosine of the angle between the two arrows. It is 1 when the arrows point the same way and 0 when they are at right angles. It measures direction only, so a long sentence and a short one can score highly when they are about the same thing.
Search by meaning A student types how do I sign up for a class. Your help page is titled course enrollment process. They share almost no words, so a keyword search, which matches the exact words typed, finds nothing useful. As positions, the two point in nearly the same direction.

The reverse also happens. How do I drop a class shares five words with the sign-up question and means the opposite, and the score reflects that. Two sentences can share many words and mean opposite things. The score here measures meaning.
Retrieval If similar meanings sit near each other, then finding the chunks of text nearest to a question becomes something a computer can calculate. This is called retrieval. It is what happens when you point an AI assistant at your documents. A student asking how to stop a double enrollment can be matched to a page about idempotency keys, which stop a repeated request from being processed twice, without the two sharing a single word.
