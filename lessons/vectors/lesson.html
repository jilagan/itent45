Vectors and similarity of meaning

A vector places a word on a map. Words used in similar ways get nearby positions, so closeness on the map stands for closeness in meaning.

On a map, two pins that are close together mark places that are near each other. A word gets a position the same way. Real models use hundreds of numbers per word, and nobody labels the words. Words that appear in similar company get similar positions.

Think first. Which other words would you want a search for enroll to find? Why might a computer place them near enroll without being told they mean the same?
Students tend to give synonyms such as register and sign up. Ask what in the text could place them close together without anyone labeling them.

Each button is a sum of three word positions: the first word, minus the second, plus the third. The circle marked result is where the sum ends up, and the nearest word is the answer. These positions were placed by hand to show the idea.

Why the bias example is disputed

A 2016 paper by Bolukbasi and colleagues reported the programmer analogy using word2vec trained on Google News, and it became a standard illustration of bias in embeddings. Nissim and colleagues showed in 2020 that the analogy method produces part of the result. The search must exclude the input words, so it has to return something other than programmer, and a gendered-looking answer is partly a product of that rule.

What holds up is smaller. An embedding is built from a corpus, and patterns in that corpus become geometry. A prompt cannot remove that, because it is built into the positions the model uses. Anyone who claims one tidy number for how biased a model is should be asked how it was measured.

One vector per use of a word

On this map, bank would get one point, somewhere between the river and the money. Newer models give a word a different vector depending on the sentence around it. That change is a main reason retrieval works as well as it does today.

Both statements hold, and students tend to keep only one of them: embeddings do absorb the statistics of their corpus, and the famous analogies overstate how cleanly that shows up.

What the score is

The score is the cosine of the angle between the two arrows. It is 1 when the arrows point the same way and 0 when they are at right angles. It measures direction only, so a long sentence and a short one can score highly when they are about the same thing.

A student types how do I sign up for a class. The help page is titled course enrollment process. The two share almost no words, so a keyword search finds nothing useful. Their positions point the same way, so finding the nearest text, called retrieval, finds the page.

Retrieval in AI assistants

Retrieval happens when you point an AI assistant at your documents. A student asking how to stop a double enrollment can be matched to a page about idempotency keys, which stop a repeated request from being processed twice, without the two sharing a single word.

The reverse case

How do I drop a class shares five words with the sign-up question and means the opposite, and the score reflects that. Two sentences can share many words and mean opposite things.
