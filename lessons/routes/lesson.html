Finding the shortest route

Many problems ask for the best way through connected places. A route has a total cost, and the cheapest one is often hard to spot by eye.

You ask a navigation app for directions from home to school. It shows two routes: one ride, or two rides with a transfer and a wait. Each ride has a cost and a number of minutes. Picking the best way through connected places is a routing problem.

Think first. One ride takes 40 minutes. Two rides take 15 and 20 minutes plus a 10 minute wait. Which is faster, and what else would you check?
The single ride is faster, 40 minutes against 45. Listen for cost, comfort and the risk of a missed transfer. These come back in the last step.

A map of stops joined by rides is a network. Each number is the minutes for that ride, and every ride costs 13 pesos. Try to reach School in under 40 minutes.

On this map the fastest route takes 38 minutes and four rides. The route with the fewest rides takes 43 minutes and three rides, so it costs 13 pesos less. Gaining on one measure while losing on another is a trade-off. The traveler decides which measure counts more.

You can now read a map as a network, total a route's cost, and replan when a line closes.

More detail

Taking the shortest next ride

Suppose a rider takes the shortest ride from each stop and never returns to a stop already visited. On this map that rider goes Home, Stop A, Stop C, Stop B, Stop D, Stop E, School, which takes 57 minutes. Totaling whole routes finds 38.

Costs besides time

Waiting time and the risk of a missed transfer also matter. A route app can let the traveler weight them.
