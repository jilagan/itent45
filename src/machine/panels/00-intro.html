AI, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI

A calculator gives the right change because someone wrote the rules of arithmetic into it. A new cashier learns to spot a fake bill by handling thousands of bills, and nobody writes down the rule. Both are useful, and they get there in different ways. That difference separates four terms that people often use as if they meant the same thing: artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, deep learning and generative AI. Each one sits inside the one before it.

Knowing which term fits a system tells you how it was built and how it tends to fail.

Think first. A chess program beat the world champion in 1997. Would you call it artificial intelligence? Did it learn from the games it played? Decide on your answers, then check them against the chess example below.
Ask for a show of hands on both questions before students open the chess example. Most will say yes to AI and split on learning. The rule-based case matters most for the rest of the module, because later panels assume students can tell a learned model from a hand-written program.

Two of these systems are AI that does no machine learning at all. Many people forget that this kind exists.

Here are the four terms from the outside in. Three terms need defining first. A feature is one measurement that a person decides to feed the program, such as the number of links in an email. Labeled data means examples that come with the right answer attached, such as emails already marked spam or not spam. An algorithm is a fixed set of steps for solving a problem.

Outermost
Artificial intelligence

Any program that does something we would call intelligent if a person did it, such as searching, planning, following rules or reasoning with logic. Most of it was written by hand, learns nothing, and is decades old.

chess engines · route finding · scheduling
Inside that
Machine learning

Programs that work out their own rule from examples. Nobody writes the rule down. People supply a million emails already labeled spam or not spam, and the program finds the dividing line between the two groups itself.

spam filters · credit scoring · recommendations
Inside that
Deep learning

Machine learning built from many stacked layers. The layers work out for themselves what is worth measuring, so a person no longer chooses the features. You give it the raw input, such as pixels or sound.

face recognition · speech to text · translation
Innermost
Generative AI

Systems that produce new content, such as a paragraph, an image or a working function. A spam filter gives one of two answers, spam or not spam. A generative system writes out something that did not exist before.

language models · image models · coding assistants
Three points to keep in mind AI is not new. The term was coined in 1956. What changed recently is the small innermost circle in the diagram.

Most AI does not learn. A great deal of what runs your bank, your airline and your traffic lights is rules that people wrote. It works, and nobody markets it as AI any more.

When a product says "AI" today, it usually means the innermost circle. Ask which kind it is, because the four fail in different ways. A rule that is wrong is wrong the same way every time. A model that is wrong is wrong in a way that depends on the examples it was trained on.

The rest of this module is about the innermost circle. It covers what the model predicts, how it is trained, what it reads, and what happens when you give it tools.
